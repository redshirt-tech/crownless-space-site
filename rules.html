Operations Manual

Setting Primer — The Cooperative

Introduction to Crownless Space

The year is 2300AD. It's been 20 years since VZ Day, the end of the war against the Zookeeper machine swarms that threatened to destroy humanity and for millennia has been preventing sentient life in the region from exploring the galaxy. Humanity was assisted in this effort by the Lyrans, a civilization who had been driven away from their own home by these same extermination machines, and who found a new home in the Sol system.

Together, Humans and Lyrans were able to mount a defense of Sol, and eventually mobilized to clear the nearby star systems of the Zookeeper menace, meeting several other civilizations in the process. The war was long, brutal, and costly, but it led to the birth of the Interstellar Cooperative, now numbering billions of members across dozens of species and star systems.

The Cooperative adheres above all else to a set of principles, the Protocols, that oblige members to work in the interests of the membership. The Cooperative framework does not allow for inequality, artificial scarcity, or unnecessary suffering across its membership. Members have a duty to one another, and to share in the common prosperity generated by the collective efforts of the membership.

Although the Cooperative has become more concerned with internal development since the end of the war, there is a growing need for an interstellar presence to maintain security and prosperity, as well as manage relationships with nearby interstellar civilizations. The Cooperative Interstellar Service Fleet operates the orbital, interplanetary, and interstellar ships and infrastructure of the Cooperative, coordinated by High Command.

High Command has put out a call for volunteers willing to command deep space missions. Now more than any time since the Great Mobilization there is a need for capable individuals ready to serve at the frontier between the known and unknown. Great adventure, danger, and opportunity await those brave enough to do their Duty at the edge of the known universe.

Being in command of a deep space vessel is one of the most difficult jobs in the Cooperative, with extremely high stakes, grave responsibilities, and proportionally modest rewards. Commanders are expected to lead from the front, putting themselves in danger on behalf of the crew. However, commanding a ship offers unique opportunities for those seeking novelty, adventure, challenge, recognition, redemption, or as a stepping stone to some greater purpose. Successful commanders gain experience, prestige, and credibility that can distinguish them greatly from their peers. If nothing else, the risk and hardship is one of the highest forms of duty to the Cooperative.

Humanity's History: From Ruin to the Stars

The Collapse

In the late 21st century, humanity had hit a breaking point. Efforts to curb looming environmental, political, and economic disaster had fallen short, and the world descended into chaos.

The population halved during the wars, and halved again in the aftermath. The elites had created an escape plan for themselves, launching a series of arks into space on newly-built fusion torch ships, off to seek a new home, or perhaps just wait out the disaster. Whether they succeeded in their mission is unknown; they were never heard from again.

The Founding of the Cooperative

Of those left behind, some took it upon themselves to rally the remains of humanity under a new banner. The International Cooperative was formed, a coalition of communities based not on distributing rights and privileges, but on duty and shared responsibility to humanity. A set of protocols was adopted to maintain cohesion across many disparate communities. Those who bound themselves to the protocols would dedicate themselves solely to the wellbeing rest of the membership, and those who refused were left to their own devices.

The strategy proved effective. While warlords, bandits, and the leftover remains of old nations fought amongst each other for advantage, the Cooperative built a new society united by shared struggle and shared prosperity. The fractured remains of the old world couldn't compete, and whether out of ideology or necessity, the Cooperative gradually absorbed the remnants of human civilization. A generation after the depths of the collapse, and for the first time in history, there was a single, unified guiding principle for all of humanity.

The New Renaissance

The following century saw a steady climb from hardship and loss towards a global renaissance. The ecosystem rebounded, industry was rebuilt, technology was rediscovered and pushed yet further. Humanity flourished as it had never done before. At the pinnacle of this resurgence was the unlocking of the Grand Unified Theory of physics, which pointed to the existence of magnetic monopole matter in the earth's crust.

Monopolium, or magmatter, is the basis for a number of miraculous technological advances, most notably in devices that generate negative energy, the key to faster than light communication and travel. However, developing these technologies gained humanity the attention of the Zookeepers.

The Zookeepers

The Zookeepers (a reference to the Zookeeper hypothesis, a solution to the Fermi Paradox) are autonomous, self-replicating machines who lie dormant, waiting to detect signs of civilizations that are on the cusp of interstellar travel. When they do, they move to destroy the civilization's interstellar capabilities, often wiping them out entirely. Why they do this is a matter of great debate, but they seem to be a ubiquitous presence throughout the galaxy.

When humanity reached out to the stars with their FTL transmitter, the Zookeeper listening station hidden in the depths of the Oort Cloud began to assemble an extermination swarm bound for Earth. They would certainly have succeeded, if not for the arrival of the Lyran migration fleet.

The Lyrans

The Lyrans are a civilization from a star system in the Lyra constellation. They were given a warning about the Zookeepers from a message buried deep below their planet's surface. Through careful planning, they managed to quietly build a migration fleet to move their population off-world before the Zookeepers could assemble the necessary force to wipe them out. After the exodus, the Lyran home planet was razed and irradiated, and the Zookeeper extermination fleet began pursuit of the Lyran migration.

The Zookeepers were relentless, but slow. As non-sentient machines, they lack the ability to sense the multidimensional realm, preventing them from being able to access true FTL travel. The Lyrans were safe, but only as long as their ships would last.

First Contact

After decades of running and hiding from the Zookeepers, the Lyran fleet detected a negative energy signature from Earth, a sign of a sophisticated technological society. The Lyrans made haste to Earth, hoping they would find a civilization that had overcome the Zookeeper threat. Unfortunately, they found a defenseless civilization under attack by a faceless enemy with unknown motives.

The Lyrans arrived as the first machine swarms were cleansing the Solar system of human presence. The Earth was pummeled with asteroid debris and saw millions of casualties in the first moments of the attack, but the bombardment stopped all of a sudden as the Lyran fleet formed around the planet. The Zookeeper swarm was designed to eliminate human civilization, but was unprepared for thousands of Lyran ships arriving at superluminal speeds. Within hours, the swarm had been destroyed, and the Lyrans made formal contact with humanity.

The Looming Threat

Humanity was deeply grateful to the Lyrans, but the two civilizations were still in danger. The Zookeepers would have alerted other swarms to the situation and would be sending a response. Such a response would take years or even decades to arrive, but it would be inevitable and overwhelming. There were two options: fight or flee.

The Lyran ships were starting to wear, and wouldn't be suitable for another decades-long journey. Humanity would not consider leaving the home that they had only just recently reclaimed from the brink of ruin. They would stand and fight. The Lyrans, being the newcomers and relatively few in number, chose to reform their leadership and formally join the Cooperative, transforming it from an international organization to interstellar.

The Great Mobilization

The Lyrans were only a few million in their migration fleet, but they had advanced technology and substantial experience with space travel. Humanity was less advanced, but they numbered in the billions and were highly adaptable. They formed a generation-long plan to industrialize the Solar system, train a fighting force, and build a steady stream of warships to meet the Zookeeper swarm that was already on the way.

They built space elevators and asteroid mining operations, the orbital ring around Earth, the magmatter extraction facility floating in the atmosphere of Jupiter, the L5 shipyard. Every soul in the system had a job to do, designing, building, operating, maintaining, and supporting the war effort. Ships, drones, missiles, and orbital defense platforms were built by the megaton. Technology was pushed to new heights, advanced beyond anything humans or Lyrans had ever produced. As they built, they detected more and more swarms approaching Earth at nearly the speed of light, and they had to build faster still.

The War

The Zookeepers began to arrive in waves, and Cooperative forces were sent to meet them. Losses were high at first, but the fleet learned, adapted, and eventually turned the tide. The machine swarms were dispatched faster than they could arrive, and so the immediate survival of the people of the Cooperative was secure.

The Solar system was cleared of threats, but new signals kept popping up on long-range sensors. The Cooperative knew they would need to extend the fight further. They built huge gateway ships to project battle fleets into nearby star systems, digging out entrenched Zookeepers to stop them from multiplying, and jamming their signals to keep them from alerting their distant allies. It was bloody, painstaking work, but system by system, the Zookeeper threat was brought to heel.

The Interstellar Cooperative

In the process of clearing star systems, the Cooperative encountered several other civilizations who either hadn't yet run afoul of the Zookeepers, or had been attacked by them long ago and never again attempted to leave their star system. The Cooperative made contact with these civilizations to inform them of the situation, explaining that the Cooperative fleets would need to have a presence in their systems for some time to ensure the threat to Earth and other civilizations was eliminated, but that otherwise the Cooperative had peaceful intentions.

The Cooperative established transportation links between these nearby star systems, enabling trade, migration, and cultural exchange. The fleet coordinated relief and development efforts to help stabilize precarious conditions and raise the overall standard of living for these peoples, following the spirit of the protocols that governed the Cooperative. Throughout the process, many of these civilizations requested membership in the Cooperative, and many individuals from these liberated planets would join the Cooperative fleet in service to the cause.

The Assembly

With the end of the war in sight, a new formal interstellar government was established. The Assembly of Planets allowed for coordination of trade and development efforts across the growing Cooperative. As its first official act, the Assembly ratified the original Cooperative Protocols as the basis for law across the Cooperative membership.

The Assembly would leave sovereignty to planetary governments, but would have jurisdiction over interstellar activity and security as a shared concern. Fleet operations would continue with the existing fleet command structure until the end of the war, at which point control would be transitioned to a newly-formed High Command, under the authority of the Assembly. The war would continue as long as needed, but civilian governments would plan for peace.

Swords to Plowshares

As the last of the Zookeeper positions were destroyed, the need for weapons and soldiers waned. The vast Solar industrial defense complex was repurposed toward development of the new Cooperative member civilizations. Some were living in pre-industrial or post-apocalyptic societies, and those with more robust terrestrial industrial development had only basic space-based infrastructure. There was a need to ensure that all planets in the Cooperative had the basic infrastructure required to participate in the Cooperative as peers, which required more than defense against the Zookeepers.

Warships were either retrofitted for civilian purposes, decommissioned and stripped for parts, or broken down entirely and turned into material for terrestrial infrastructure. Gateway ships were converted into permanent transit hubs to allow for safe, efficient flow of goods and people throughout the Cooperative. Many fleet personnel retired from active duty and joined the peacetime efforts to strengthen the Cooperative, or simply found a place to quietly live their lives.

VZ Day and Beyond

By the time the last Zookeeper swarm was eliminated, most people were only peripherally aware of the war, having long-since seen the Zookeeper incursions get further away and fewer between for years. A day of celebration was declared to welcome service members home, honor retired veterans, mourn the dead, and declare the beginning of a new era.

There would undoubtedly be more contact with Zookeepers, and surely there were unknown threats that awaited them in the vastness of deep space, but the people of the Cooperative were finally free of the ticking clock that many of them had known since birth.

Today, 20 years on, the Cooperative has new challenges and new opportunities as their membership spreads further across the galaxy.

Life in the Cooperative

The vast majority of the membership lives terrestrially, on the surface of a planet or moon with a natural habitable environment. There are dense urban communities, small rural towns, nomadic peoples, isolated hermitages, homesteaders, and a few lone wanderers. All have unconditional access to basic services and infrastructure, although there is still inherently a gap between what's available in densely populated urban areas compared more remote locations. Still, many prefer being closer to nature, or further from the hustle and bustle of city life, and most members find their way to their preferred way of life.

Member worlds are connected by an interstellar tachyonic communications network for instant communication across light years. Some star systems host a waygate, a connection to the transit system that connects points in space via wormholes, obviating the need for most members to physically cross the large and often dangerous areas between inhabited systems. The Cooperative strives to eventually to connect all member star systems together via the waygate network, but the infrastructure is expensive to build and maintain, requiring a great deal of specialized equipment and personnel, and so the connections between worlds is an ongoing project.

Cooperative worlds vary in environment, population, and level of development, but the Cooperative strives to maintain a similar level of security and quality of life for members on each world. Dirty industry has largely moved off-planet, natural disasters are mitigated by careful monitoring and judicious technological intervention, and people have ready access to infrastructure and services that meet their wants and needs. Automation is the norm, and labour is applied only where it is necessary or as desired.

Day-to-day life is, in many ways, the same as it has always been. Automation has eliminated the need for tedious, but there is still great demand for the technicians, engineers, and scientists who can operate, maintain, and improve on those automated systems. And of course there are the areas where automation is limited or antithetical to the purpose. Educators, care workers, artists, designers, craftspeople, bureaucrats, chefs, academics, and so on. People find purpose in mastery of skills, building of relationships, achievement of goals, and contribution to their communities.

Conflict also arises in the ways it always has. People have differing goals, values, priorities, loyalties, opinions, etc. Competition and rivalry are still alive and well, even if they are rarely existential. While scarcity on an individual level is largely a solved problem, the universe still imposes finite limits while offering infinite possibilities.

On top of that, other civilizations are becoming increasingly aware of the Cooperative, and with that awareness comes new challenges. Many in the membership wish to remain removed from the dangers and risks that come from elsewhere in the galaxy, but others believe that these challenges cannot be ignored if their hard-won peace and harmony are to be preserved.

The Burden of Command

The Burden of Command

The players will be taking on the role of the commanders of a newly-chartered deep space mission. You will be given authority over a recently-refurbished starship, with the responsibility to uphold the Protocols, keep your crew safe, and fulfill your stated mission.

As commanders, your job is to decide where your ship goes, what activities you prioritize, and how you engage with the rest of the galaxy. You will often be alone, hours or days away from reinforcements, sometimes even out of communications range, and so you will have broad latitude to exercise your discretion in the field.

Further, since the war ended, deep space missions are less common, as most service fleet members opt to serve closer to home. Your services will be in high demand, and you will receive regular requests from the various fleet divisions who have ample work available for capable crews.

The Mission

The first step is to determine an overall mission for the ship, and condense it into a single mission statement. The mission can be as specific or as broad as you choose, and is intended to be something that the players share as a goal, even as the individual PCs may have their own motivations and objectives. Some questions to consider:

  • How do you want to change the galaxy?
  • What sorts of missions do you want to take on?
  • What goals, values, and objectives do you want to prioritize?
  • How do you want to motivate your crew?
  • How will you know you're fulfilling your mission?

You are free to change your mission mid-campaign if conditions change or you feel there are more important concerns, but we have to start somewhere.

Example: "To explore new worlds, seek out new life and new civilizations, to boldly go where no one has gone before."

Command Structure

While the PCs will be part of a quasi-military organization, and there will be a need to make quick decisions in-game on behalf of the ship and crew, the meta-game reality is that the players will be making decisions as a party by some form of consensus. To that end, the players will need to decide how the ship is being run within the game. You could choose one among you to be the captain, take turns, make decisions by committee, or you can elect to have an NPC captain who "gives orders" in-game but is really just a conduit for group decisions.

You can run your ship however you like, as long as you have a way to make decisions together. You can choose to include NPC crewmembers as you see fit, but ultimately the players are in charge of the ship, crew, and mission. The only caveat is that High Command may occasionally give you direct orders in an emergency or as a disciplinary action.

Duty and Requisition

The Cooperative uses a distributed cybernetic economic coordination framework, CyberCon, that strives for an optimal ratio of resource management to individual wellbeing through maximal membership participation in Cooperative affairs. The vast majority of production decisions and transactions are handled autonomously, with members contributing as they are able, and taking their share in the form of goods, services, and access to amenities.

As commanders of a new chartered mission, you will have a certain amount of Requisition to build your ship and hire your crew. Requisition obligates you to fulfill an equivalent amount of Duty every cycle. Cycles are based on Lunar orbits of 28 days.

Fulfilling Duty can come in many forms. The four Fleet divisions will have missions for you to accomplish in exchange for Duty fulfillment. Making discoveries, eliminating threats, upholding the Protocols in heroic ways, or generally advancing the goals of the Cooperative will also count towards Duty fulfilled. Duty fulfilled above your quota can be carried forward to the following cycle, or used to increase your Service Rating, which increases the amount of Requisition you have available. This will also increase your Duty requirements by an equivalent amount. To whom much is given, much is expected.

The Ship and Crew

Your ship is a refurbished Kodiak-class warship. These ships were mothballed after the Zookeeper war for being unsuited to most peacetime activities. However, due to their overengineered power system and single-piece, Solarian-made hyperdiamond superstructure, they have more recently been repurposed as long-range exploration and patrol ships.

The ship was stripped of its numerous advanced weapons systems, heat sinks, beam capacitors, redundant shield arrays, and so on, but the refit saw it get a new protomatter reactor and state of the art FTL torii drive engines. Plus, removing the various advanced systems has left plenty of space for cargo. The ship has a basic beam weapon, shield array, and all the necessary amenities for the crew (although the accommodations are spartan). Your ship and crew can be upgraded with additional Requisition.

The Protocols

The Protocols

Officially titled The Cooperative Association of Sentient Beings, the Cooperative isn't an empire or government, but rather a series of shared protocols and infrastructure that spans many star systems, planets, species, organizations, and individuals. While there is tremendous variety in the way the Cooperative chooses to manifest itself, the following provides a shared baseline that allows for collaboration toward shared security and prosperity.

Below are the seven core principles of the Cooperative Protocols, the basis for law and convention within the Cooperative membership and its associated territories:

  • Sacrifice — The needs of the many outweigh the needs of the few. Members have a duty to the greater good of the membership.
  • Aid — The needs of one are the responsibility of all. The membership has a duty to provide for each member.
  • Agency — Relationships must be mutually agreeable. No member may hold dominion over another sentient being.
  • Equity — Transactions must be mutually beneficial. No member may exploit another sentient being.
  • Safety — An injury to one is an injury to all. No member may harm another sentient being, except in defense of life, and members have a duty to defend other members from harm to the best of their ability.
  • Volition — Membership is voluntary and opt-in. No member may engage in any active effort to persuade or coerce individuals to join, nor to dissuade or obstruct individuals from joining. Even individuals who originate within the Cooperative must actively seek membership.
  • Care — In the case of members or non-members who have inherently limited agency, ability, opportunity, etc., members may extend a duty of care to these individuals without obligating them to strict observance of the Protocols. (Ex. children)

Membership

  • Membership in the Cooperative is open to all sentient beings. 'Sentient' in this context refers broadly to intelligent, self-aware, conscious beings.
  • Membership is on an individual basis.
  • No group may compel an individual to become a member;
  • No individual may compel a group to become members;
  • Associations, institutions, sovereign entities, and other forms of collective organization of sentient beings are not eligible for membership. They may, upon renouncing previous attachments, form new organizations and structures together within the Cooperative framework.
  • Membership is contingent only on acceptance and observance of the Protocols.
  • No member may refuse or ignore membership to an individual who observes the Protocols;
  • No individual may claim membership or its benefits without following the Protocols.
  • Membership is inalienable. It may not be transferred, nor renounced. While membership cannot be revoked, members may deprioritize Protocol obligations to members who themselves fail to observe Protocol.
  • Membership is following the Protocols, no less.

Relating to Non-members

  • Non-members are permitted free movement through Cooperative-controlled jurisdictions unless otherwise specified.
  • Non-members are expected to obey the laws and conventions of Cooperative jurisdictions.
  • Members are not obligated by the Protocols to provide non-members with material support, although members should observe the spirit of the Protocols when relating to non-members, especially those who are themselves operating in the spirit of the Protocols.
  • Members in jurisdictions controlled by non-member institutions are expected to follow the laws and conventions of those jurisdictions. Where there is direct conflict with the Protocols, the spirit of the Protocols should still be observed to the best of members' abilities.

CyberCon and Duty

Based on the original framework developed by the first Cooperative on Earth, Cooperative members have agreed to a shared resource management and service allocation system, CyberCon, that lets members share their wants and needs, share their abilities and outputs, and provide a sense of their overall situation to the larger community. This information is processed via a decentralized planning and optimization algorithm, then the outputs are assessed by the appropriate level of governance (local, regional, global, intersystem, and interstellar). They ratify the results, as well as make decisions when there are nontrivial issues with multiple potential solutions. The system then takes the feedback and codifies the results into discrete goals or responsibilities, measured as Duty. Members or organizations can then commit to fulfilling these Duties.

Members are encouraged to fulfill a certain amount of lifetime Duty as their contribution to the Cooperative, and are rewarded with additional benefits commensurate with their contribution, not to mention social recognition for service above and beyond the minimum Duty required. Benefits can be in the form of superior accommodations, luxury goods, priority access to recreational facilities, hospitality, services, and so on. Individuals often feel social pressure to fulfill at least their minimum Duty, or else can be viewed as lazy or selfish, while those who go out of their way to fulfill more Duty often accrue more respect and favorable treatment.

A very small number of members choose to perform no Duty at all and live on the minimum standard provided by the Cooperative. These members are a mix of religious practitioners, political dissidents, artists, minimalists, and the mentally ill. How they are perceived by society differs between cultures and circumstances, but unless they are actively disruptive or present a legitimate threat to themselves or others, they are tolerated, and in some cases actively supported.

Those who wish to go above and beyond their mere minimum Duty requirements can sign up for Cooperative Service, contracts that commit them fully to the Cooperative membership for some number of years. The most difficult and dangerous service commitment is that of the Cooperative Interstellar Service, making the pledge to go into space far beyond the safety and comfort of a familiar planet or even star system. But Interstellar Service is also one of the highest forms of service, and a doorway to a variety of unique opportunities inside and even outside the Cooperative.

Chartered Institutions and Projects

While the Cooperative itself has no centralized form or specific boundary, there is often a need for the organization of individuals toward shared goals and mutual understanding. To that end, the Protocols allow members to form associations for the purposes of collaboration, standardization, and mobilization at-scale or across time and space.

These associations come in the form of Institutions and Projects. Institutions are associations that are intended to provide continuity and consistency on an ongoing basis in service of a stated purpose. Projects are associations that are intended to meet a specific goal. The two are identical in function, save that Projects are dissolved after fulfilling (or failing to fulfill) their predefined mandate, whereas Institutions have an indefinite or ongoing mandate.

Each Institution and Project must have a Charter that outlines its purpose and its criteria for association. The Chartered Institution and Project (CIP) Protocols are:

  • CIPs must uphold the Protocols above all other priorities, up to and including the existence of the CIP itself.
  • CIPs endorsed by a substantial number of members and/or other CIPs, or with endorsements from enough members or CIPs in very high standing, are considered to be in good standing. Standing is directly related to both the amount of endorsements and the quality of the endorsements.
  • CIPs in good standing may be given priority consideration by members when it comes to resource allocation.
  • Members of CIPs who take upon themselves to be responsible for appropriate allocation of resources and material support for the CIP are considered charter members. They have a duty to uphold the CIP charter, and their Service Rating is affected by the CIP's success or failure to fulfill its Duty.
  • Members who pledge to provide material assistance to CIPs are considered associate members of the CIP, and associate members of a CIP are considered to be meeting their individual Duties, in proportion to assistance rendered to the CIP.
  • Members may be associates of multiple CIPs concurrently, as long as their commitments to each do not conflict.
  • CIPs that are reported to be in violation of Protocols are subject to review, which can result in public admonishment, loss of standing, and potentially dissolution. Reviews are delegated as per CIP charter. Usually reviews are delegated to local or regional authorities, but may be delegated further to appropriate bodies of governance at the discretion of the reviewers. Reviews are often delegated to the Planetary Assembly when there is a cross-jurisdictional violation, or where there is significant intra-jurisdictional conflict.
  • CIPs must originate within the Cooperative membership. Non-Cooperative entities may not become CIPs (as per Protocols). Collaboration with non-members and non-Cooperative entities is permitted, provided that CIPs follow the Protocols.
  • CIPs may implement and enforce regulations on their associated members, provided that regulations are compatible with the Protocols, in-service to the CIP charter, and are applied consistently across all associated members.
  • Non-associated members and non-members are expected to adhere to CIP regulations relating to guests while present in CIP-administered facilities. Regulations are the responsibility of chartered members, and CIPs have broad latitude as to how they set and enforce regulations, provided the Protocols are upheld as a first principle.

Organizational Service Rating

CIPs operate by having members pledge their service to them. This makes the CIP responsible for fulfilling Duty requirements for its associated members, and they in-turn commit to contribute to the Duties of the CIP. CIPs have their own Service Ratings in the form of Organizational Service Ratings, which is a rating of a CIPs overall obligation and contribution to the Cooperative. Organizational Service Rating (OSR) is similar to service rating, but takes into account the nature and degree of the CIP charter, as well as the amount of labor and infrastructure required to meet their commitments.

OSR is generally a reflection of the relative importance of an organization to the Cooperative, whether because it operates at large scale, provides critical or specialized services, and/or has a track record for effective, efficient, or exceptional contributions.

Non-member individuals may join CIPs, provided that they meet the CIP's Charter requirements and observe the Protocols while acting on behalf of the CIP. Non-members may be compensated for their service upon the discretion of the CIP, which takes responsibility for all resources requested and allocated from the Cooperative and its members to non-members.

Organizational structures of CIPs can vary widely, from pure consensus-based to strictly hierarchical, and there is no minimum or maximum size, so long as the CIP upholds the Protocols and remains in good standing.

Conflict Resolution

Members have a duty to report substantial breaches of Protocol. In the event that there is a breach of Protocol by one or more members or CIPs, it is the responsibility of members to address the breach. The degree of intervention should be dependent on the context, but the approach to conflict is the same:

  • Membership is kinship. Conflict arising between members must be resolved in the spirit of kinship.
  • Discuss before escalating. Should a conflict arise, members should first exhaust the possibility of a resolution through informal discussion. All efforts must be made to rule out misunderstanding before escalation.
  • Escalate judiciously and decisively. Should informal discussion yield no progress, formal escalation should be the minimum response required to resolve the issue.
  • Methods of escalation include:
  • Formal discussion published in the public record;
  • Formal mediation by a mutually-agreeable third-party;
  • Binding arbitration by a mutually-agreeable third-party;
  • Formal declaration of injury or loss, requiring restitution to the injured party;
  • Formal declaration of injury or loss, requiring evaluation of the accused party as a threat;
  • Formal declaration of warning or threat, which clarifies the intent to use force to prevent further breach of protocol;
  • Use of force, the degree of which should be whatever is required to decisively end a conflict with a minimum of further injury or loss.
  • The ladder of force escalation is as follows:
  • Restrain — Contain and/or control a threat without risk of harm to the threat.
  • Subdue — Neutralize a threat without risk of lasting harm.
  • Injure — Mitigate a threat by causing limited harm as a show of intent.
  • Maim — Mitigate a threat by causing lasting harm.
  • Destroy — Eliminate a threat entirely. This is a last resort and only justifiable in service to preserving life in defense of self or others.

Judiciary institutions across the cooperative are capable of providing guidance, services, and judgment based on these principles and the specifics of local jurisprudence. Should the need arise, The Assembly regularly convenes a judicial committee which oversees appeals to local judicial cases.
